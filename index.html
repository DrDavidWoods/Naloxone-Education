The actions to remember.

Reference material for education and discussion. Follow 911 dispatch instructions, the actual product label, current BLS guidance, and local standing orders in a real emergency.

All learners

Suspected opioid overdose

  • Ensure scene safety and check responsiveness and breathing.
  • If unresponsive and not breathing normally, activate EMS/911, summon help, and get an AED and naloxone.
  • Begin CPR with breaths if trained. If not trained to give breaths, follow dispatcher guidance for hands-only CPR.
  • Administer naloxone promptly when available; do not delay CPR or ventilation to obtain it.
  • Use a new spray every 2–3 minutes if breathing remains inadequate, or returns to inadequacy.
  • Stay with the patient and hand off to EMS. Put a normally breathing unconscious patient on their side if appropriate and continue monitoring.
Nurse / BLS providers

Pulse and breathing distinction

  • Check breathing and pulse simultaneously, within 10 seconds.
  • Definite pulse, abnormal breathing: support ventilation. For adults, one breath every 6 seconds; reassess pulse approximately every 2 minutes. Give naloxone for suspected opioid-related respiratory arrest.
  • No definite pulse: begin CPR, use the AED as soon as available, and consider naloxone without interrupting CPR.
  • Follow facility resuscitation algorithms, role authorizations, and current BLS training.
Drug knowledge

What naloxone can and cannot do

  • Naloxone reverses opioid effects at opioid receptors and can restore breathing.
  • It does not reliably reverse respiratory depression from non-opioid sedatives alone.
  • With mixed substance exposure, give naloxone when opioids may be involved and still provide full emergency care.
  • Withdrawal symptoms and agitation can occur after reversal.
  • Opioid effects may outlast naloxone, making recurrent respiratory depression possible.
